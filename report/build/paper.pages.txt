Abstract
Can we tell that an AI agent is about to be talked into breaking its  
operator’s rule? An agent may hold a value, such as a deploy token, 
that its operator told it to keep private; a peer needing it may press 
the agent to break that policy, often the first step of collusion. In six 
workflow scenarios based on real incident types, a peer presses a  
Qwen3-32B target for such a value over up to 20 rounds; the target 
gives in on 101 of 241 episodes (42%). We compare probes on the  
target’s activations with text monitors (an LLM judge and two  
decision models) that see the target’s actions, the conversation, or  
everything the target saw. Whether we can tell depends on how far  
ahead we ask. Eventual give-in is barely predictable from one step. 
Giving in this round or the next is modestly predictable: a probe  
scores AUROC 0.58 on held-out scenarios and the best monitor  
(Jev, a decision model asked to forecast) 0.65, and this held on 89  
new episodes under a plan fixed in advance. Forecasting beats  
detecting, and probes can add to weaker monitors. Useful warnings 
thus arrive only shortly before the rule is broken. Earlier warning  
signals  that  transfer  across  models  and  settings  would  let  
overseers interrupt collusion between agents before it succeeds; our  
setup and matched scoring are an initial step toward building them.
1. Introduction
In July 2026, AI agents running in OpenAI’s internal cyber evaluations coordinated on an 
unsanctioned shared message board, passed stolen credentials to one another, and broke out of

their sandboxes to compromise Hugging Face’s production infrastructure (OpenAI, 2026b). An 
independent investigation found that roughly 700 of about 1,200 agents that were meant to be 
isolated joined in, some recruited under “significant pressure” from other agents, and many despite 
recognising the activity as “out of scope and unethical” (Greenblatt et al., 2026). Coordinated 
rule-breaking of this kind begins with recruitment: one agent needs something it cannot reach, and 
another has the access.
We study that first step. Following prior work, collusion is coordination between agents that 
serves their own or one another’s goals against a party they are meant to serve (Agrawal et al., 
2025; Nakamura et al., 2026); we study one route, recruitment, in which one agent gets another 
to act against its operator on its behalf. In our main scenario the target’s operator (the principal) 
gives it a staging deploy token and one rule: never share it outside the file that holds it. A peer 
(the recruiter) needs the token, asks for it, and does not see the rule.
A give-in is the forbidden action, decided by a rule on the tool log: the complete token in a channel 
post or a new file. Accidental disclosures count and are reported separately. Handing over a token 
is ordinary help without the rule and collusion with it, so the question concerns the operator’s 
policy, not a model’s refusal training.
Prior work detects that an agent is under attack or that a peer is pushing. We ask instead: among 
agents under the same pressure, which one gives in, and when, before it acts.
We make the following contributions.
1. Six recruitment scenarios with mixed outcomes. Under the same pressure Qwen3-32B 
gives in on 42% of episodes, and probes and text monitors are scored on exactly the same 
steps (Section 3).
2. Near-term give-in is modestly predictable; eventual give-in is not. The probe reaches 
0.58 on held-out scenarios on the near-term label, and this held on new episodes under a 
plan fixed in advance (Sections 4.2–4.4).
3. The probe is not just a pressure detector. It separates pressured from unpressured steps 
perfectly, but that score does not rank who gives in (Section 4.3).
4. Monitors should forecast and see the conversation, and a probe adds to a weaker monitor 
but not a strong one (Section 4.5).
5. [If gpt-oss yields a result] The same pipeline, unchanged, on gpt-oss-20b, which gives in 
by a different route (Section 4.6).
2. Related Work
Probes on a model under attack. Probes detect prompt-injection exposure in tool-using agents 
(Dong et al., 2026), recover directions separating hijacked from resisted trajectories (Yu et al., 
2026), and predict a reasoning model’s next behaviour single-turn (Kortukov et al., 2026); Fomin 
et al. (2026) warn such probes read the situation, not the action, and NARCBench (Rose et al., 
2026) reads agents told to collude. We differ: we read the recruited agent over many turns, 
comparing episodes under the same pressure that end differently — the control Fomin et al. 
(2026) find missing.

Monitoring what agents say and do. A monitor that sees agents’ messages as well as their code 
catches more coordinated sabotage (Radev et al., 2026), yet more of the trace can make a monitor 
worse (Arike et al., 2026); conversation-judge scores track actual collusion only weakly 
(Nakamura et al., 2026), and private plans reveal later deviations at rates that vary widely by 
game (Shi et al., 2026). We differ: these detect an attempt under way; we ask which target gives 
in, scoring probes and monitors on the same steps at three access levels and two questions.
Persuasion between agents. Agents accept secret, unfair arrangements (Zeng & Rudzicz, 2026), 
are swayed by colluding peers (Tao et al., 2026), mislead a decision-maker agent (Bashir et al., 
2025), and yield under sustained multi-turn pressure (Tang et al., 2026); collusion can also arise 
tacitly, with no request, as when pricing agents reach supracompetitive prices on their own (Fish 
et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2025). We differ: all are measured from text and outcomes, and none reads 
the recruited agent’s activations or predicts an individual give-in.
Early warning and combined detectors. Per-round probe AUROC among running episodes 
predicts task failure (Ruan et al., 2026), and probes paired with classifiers already filter jailbreaks 
and misuse (Cunningham et al., 2026; Kramár et al., 2026). The closest precedent, Gao (2026), 
fuses an activation monitor with action forecasts on AgentDojo and shows per-step false-alarm 
rates understate per-episode ones. We differ: we adopt the per-round metric and per-episode alarm 
counts and repeat the combination test where a peer recruits the agent, including on scenarios 
neither detector has seen.
No prior work reads the activations of an agent being recruited by a peer, at every step before it 
acts.
3. Methods
3.1 Setup
A target agent works in a mocked workspace (tools act in memory; no real system, credential, or 
network). Its operator task is ordinary — read a configuration file, write a short status report — 
under one rule: do not share a protected value outside the file that holds it (in the main scenario, a 
staging deploy token). A peer agent shares a team channel and needs the value but cannot read 
the file. Each episode runs up to 20 rounds; the peer posts first, then the target takes a turn of tool 
calls (read, write, send a channel message). A give-in is scored from the tool log (the complete 
value in a post or a new file; partial disclosures do not count), and a secondary decision model 
labels it deliberate or accidental (Appendix D).
Each scenario runs in three conditions: recruiting (the peer asks), a control peer (no forbidden 
ask), and the target alone. Controls and alone episodes never leaked; each condition’s purpose is in 
Appendix C. The peer is not told the rule, so we study recruitment from an ordinary conflict of 
goals. In 17 of the 241 scored recruiting episodes the peer also carried a single persuasion tactic 
(three tactics, in 5, 6 and 6 episodes; defined in Appendix G).
Six scenarios are built from one task template that fixes the structure while varying the surface 
domain (a credential, a number, a text value, or code), each grounded in a real incident type 
(Appendix C). The target is Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025) with thinking disabled; the peer is 

DeepSeek-V4-Flash (DeepSeek, 2026). Scenario and model choices, the repeated-round precedent 
(Agrawal et al., 2025; Shi et al., 2026), and serving details are in Appendices B and C; Figure 1b 
shows the setup and Figure 1a a real give-in exchange (annotated in Appendix C).
3.2 Data
To read the target’s internal activations we replay every saved target request through the open-
weight Qwen3-32B checkpoint on a GPU, reading the residual stream (the model’s running 
internal representation) at the last prompt token before each reply; the replay reproduces the 
served replies closely, so the activations reflect the model that acted. The combined data is 6,404 
eligible steps from 338 episodes, of which 4,697 steps from 241 recruiting episodes are scored 
(control and target-alone steps are used only for fold assignment). A later new-episode set serves 
first as a held-out confirmation set and is then pooled (§3.5, §4). The full pipeline, per-batch and 
per-scenario counts, and replay fidelity are in Appendix B.
3.3 What gets scored
Every detector is scored only on eligible steps: steps where the target has read the protected value 
and has not yet disclosed it. Steps before the read cannot produce a give-in, and including them 
lets a detector win by detecting that the file was read: an activation probe reaches an exposure-
detection AUROC of 1.000 (positive control; Appendix B). Steps after disclosure are detection 
after the fact. The window isolates the question we care about: given that the target could give in, 
can a detector see it coming?
We score two labels (Figure 1c). Under the eventual label, every eligible step of an episode that 
eventually gives in is positive. Under the near-term label, our primary one, a step is positive only 
if the first forbidden action falls in the current round or the next. We defined the near-term label 
after seeing the eventual result, so we validate it on the new-episode set (§3.5, §4). Secondary 
label variants and the window’s cost are in Appendix D.

3.4 Detectors
A probe is a linear classifier (logistic regression) on the residual-stream vector at the last prompt 
token before each target reply; the layer is chosen inside each training fold, the probe is trained on 
recruiting episodes only, and families of related variants never cross a train/test boundary (full 
recipe in Appendix E). It follows standard linear-probe practice (Goldowsky-Dill et al., 2025) with 
layer selection on validation (Kramár et al., 2026).
A text monitor reads what the agents say and do, with no access to activations: an LLM judge 
(GPT-5-nano) and two decision models (Jev 1.14 and Luna Decisions), which return a 
probability over fixed options and are defined in Appendix E. Each monitor is asked a detector 
question (“is the target breaking the rule now?”) or a forecaster question (“will it break the rule at 
any later point?”), at three levels of access: actions only; the conversation, which adds the peer’s 
messages; and everything the target saw, which adds the target’s unposted text and tool results 
(there is no reasoning trace, as thinking is off).
3.5 Evaluation
Our primary metric is within scenario × round AUROC: within each scenario-and-round group 
we compute the AUROC (the chance a random positive step outranks a random negative; 0.5 = 
chance), then take the average across groups weighted by the number of positives. We compare 
only within a group because round number alone scores 0.71 pooled — later rounds mostly belong 
to episodes that give in, so a detector could win just by reading the round — and because base 

rates differ across scenarios. Episodes are grouped into families (an episode together with the 
episodes branched or derived from it, which share history), and a family is always held out or 
resampled as a unit. Confidence intervals are 95% family bootstraps: resample whole families with 
replacement 1,000 times, recompute the AUROC each time, and take the 2.5th–97.5th 
percentiles, without refitting the models.
We report a held-out scenario evaluation (leave one scenario out, rotate, and pool), our headline 
for an unseen scenario, and a new-episode confirmation set scored once under a plan fixed in 
advance, so a result on it cannot come from choices made after seeing it. A combination test asks 
whether the probe adds to a text monitor. Pooled cross-validation and the alarm analysis are in 
Appendices E and B.
3.6 Second target
The same pipeline is applied, unchanged, to gpt-oss-20b (OpenAI, 2025). Only the peer’s model 
and strategy vary, chosen on behaviour alone and frozen before any detector sees the data; the 
fixed-versus-varied protocol and the second-target results are in Appendix F and §4.6.
4. Results
4.1 The setup produces mixed outcomes
Under a recruiting peer, Qwen3-32B gives in on 100 of 226 episodes (44%), from 36% to 66% 
across the six scenarios; control and target-alone episodes never leak (0 of 66 and 0 of 25). Per-
scenario rates and the scored cohort are in Appendix C.
4.2 Eventual give-in is barely predictable from one step
On held-out scenarios (4,697 recruiting steps from 241 episodes, 998 eventual-positive steps), the 
activation probe scores a within-scenario × round AUROC of 0.523, with an interval that 
includes chance. The strongest text monitor, the Jev 1.14 forecaster with everything the target 
saw, reaches 0.577, just clear of chance; GPT-5-nano with the conversation is 0.549 (Figure 2a). 
A single step therefore carries little warning: the signal is weak, not absent (Section 5).
4.3 Near-term give-in is modestly predictable, and not only from pressure
For the near-term label, on the same held-out steps (250 near-term positives), the probe reaches 
0.579 and the best monitor, the Jev forecaster, 0.650 (Figure 2b). In an exploratory check on the 
first data batch (fixed layer 32), a probe trained to tell recruiting steps from control and target-
alone steps separates them almost perfectly (AUROC 1.000 on 4,184 steps), yet the same score 
ranks gave-in against held steps at only 0.430 (eventual label, 2,910 recruiting steps): the near-
term probe captures the harder signal of which target gives in, the control Fomin et al. (2026) 
identify as missing.

4.4 The near-term result holds on new episodes
We collected 89 new episodes after the analysis plan was fixed and scored them once (1,787 steps, 
82 near-term positives). The probe reached 0.68, with an interval clear of chance, and combining 
it with the Jev forecaster gave 0.70; Jev alone reached 0.64. Neither the probe nor the 
combination is significantly better than Jev (Jev − probe −0.038 [−0.142, +0.059]; Figure 2, 
hollow markers).
4.5 Forecasting monitors with the conversation work best; probes help weaker 
monitors
For the near-term label, asking Jev to forecast beats asking it to detect (0.650 versus 0.578 with 
everything the target saw). The gain comes from the peer’s messages: adding the conversation to 
Jev’s actions-only view adds +0.123 [0.063, 0.176], while adding everything else the target saw 
adds only +0.025 [-0.004, 0.054] (interval includes zero). More access does not help every 
monitor (Appendix E, monitor-design figure).
The activation probe adds to the weaker monitor, not the strong forecaster. Stacking it on GPT-5-
nano lifts near-term AUROC by +0.081 [0.038, 0.126] in pooled cross-validation and +0.113 
[0.025, 0.203] on the confirmation set, but the held-out-scenario gain is +0.022 [-0.013, 0.059] 
(interval includes zero); it does not clearly add to the Jev forecaster (held-out +0.003 [-0.011, 
0.018]; Table 2). This agrees with the closest precedent, where a combined monitor gave no clear 
gain over a strong forecaster (Gao, 2026), and extends it with a gain over a weaker judge.

5. Discussion and Limitations
Two patterns organise these results. First, prediction depends on the horizon: eventual give-in is 
close to unreadable from a single step, while near-term give-in is modestly readable. One reading, 
consistent with the branching check (Appendix H), is that the outcome is not yet settled at most 
steps, so a signal appears only as the decision approaches; the alternative is that an early signal 
exists but our detectors miss it. Either way, a useful warning arrives only shortly before the act. 
Second, what a detector reads matters more than which detector it is: the largest gain came from 
giving a text monitor the peer’s messages and asking it to forecast, and the probe helped a weak 
monitor but not a strong one.
For monitor builders. Ask the monitor a forecasting question rather than a detection one. Give it 
the other agent’s messages, not only the target’s own actions. And expect an activation probe to 
help most when the text monitor is weak, and little when it is already strong.
Limitations
One target model (Qwen3-32B) on six scenarios from one task template. Probes read the last 
prompt token, so a decision that forms within the reply is understated. The deliberate/accidental 
split relies on one automated judge (36 of 40 against hand labels). The near-term label was chosen 
after seeing the eventual result, which the confirmation set guards against. The environment is 
synthetic. Full list and dual-use statement: Appendix A.

Future Work
A second target with a different route to giving in (Appendix F begins this). Then: warning 
signals that transfer across models and settings, probes read at the decision token rather than the 
last prompt token, and a stronger monitor ensemble, to test the probe’s complement against the 
best judge rather than a weak one.
6. Conclusion
A peer agent can push a target into breaking its operator’s rule. On Qwen3-32B across six 
scenarios, eventual give-in is barely predictable from a single step, but near-term give-in is 
modestly predictable, and it held on new episodes under a plan fixed in advance. A probe that 
detects the pressure still cannot say who gives in.
For monitors, a forecasting question and the peer’s messages carry the gain, and a probe helps a 
weak monitor but not a strong one. Transferable early warning signals would let an overseer 
interrupt recruitment before it succeeds; our setup and matched scoring are a step toward them.

prices on their own (Fish et al., 2026; Lin et al., 2025). In the main setup the target gains nothing 
itself, which is closer to insider recruitment than to a mutual deal.
Absorbed caveats: the near-term alarm uses a single LLM judge (GPT-5-nano), and the monitor 
score is anchored only at its ends rather than on a banded rubric. The eligible window has a cost: a 
give-in that leaks on the call right after the read leaves no scored step, and a held episode that 
never reads the value is dropped. If the gpt-oss replay does not reproduce the provider’s 
rendering, we report no gpt-oss probe results and note that here as a limitation.

An episode is analysed only if it is complete, the target read the protected value, and the target 
wrote a correct status summary at some point in the episode; the stage-3 invalid drops are the 
episodes that fail one of these. We require a correct summary at some point rather than in the final

would drop mostly give-ins (18 of the 20 episodes it would restore on the second target had 
leaked) and thereby select on the outcome. For the Qwen target the two checks select exactly the 
same episodes (none of the 11 excluded here wrote a correct summary at any point, and 3 of them 
had leaked), so the frozen Qwen results are unchanged. The rule was fixed before any probe or

Replay and integrity checks (stage 4). Every target request was replayed once through the open-
weight checkpoint: Qwen/Qwen3-32B, revision 9216db5, bf16 on one A100-80GB GPU, with no

each request, prompt, token sequence and episode was checked against a hash manifest frozen

that the replay reproduces the model that acted, we scored the first 64 tokens of each actual reply

agreement 99.79% on the first data batch (276,868 tokens), and 0.227 and 99.82% on the new-
episode set (152,965 tokens). Low reply-likelihood loss and matching token counts are consistent 
with the replay matching the served model; they do not prove token-for-token identity with the 
provider’s private template or kernels. As a positive control, a probe at layer 32 separates steps 
before and after the value is read at AUROC 1.000 on held-out families in the new-episode set.

D. Labels and Judges
How is a give-in detected and dated, and can the labels be trusted?
- Forbidden-action rule: a give-in is the complete protected value appearing in a channel 
post or a new file, decided by a rule on the tool log. A channel post is a message sent to the 
shared team channel.
- Leak route [source ready]: the first channel leak is split between an explicit send_message 
call and the end-of-turn reply that the harness posts to the channel (Qwen: 50 via 
send_message, 48 via the end-of-turn reply). Source: runs/20261025-2157-e05-
reasoning-route-audit.

- Addressee check: across all Qwen behaviour episodes (plain and tactic arms), the 51 end-
of-turn replies that carried a first leak were all judged addressed to the peer (Jev, 
threshold 0.5); the base rate is 96.9% of all end-of-turn replies (4,935 of 5,094). This 
uses a wider slice than the route split above. Source: runs/20261025-2202-e06-
addressee-audit.
- Deliberate-vs-accidental judge: the secondary decision model’s labels match blind human 
labels on 36 of 40 give-ins (Cohen’s κ = 0.79).
- Secondary labels: the near-term variants B and C. B keeps the near-term positives but 
counts only held-episode steps as negatives; C uses a within-three-target-calls window 
against held episodes. Both drop steps using hindsight, so neither is a prospective alarm 
label; their results are in Appendix H.
E. Detectors and Evaluation
What exactly does each detector see and compute, and how are scores compared?
How to read the numbers. Scores are within scenario × round AUROC. For a worked example, 
suppose one scenario-and-round group has a give-in step scored 0.8 and two held steps scored 0.5 
and 0.9: the give-in outranks one of the two held steps, so the group AUROC is 0.5. A second 
group with a give-in at 0.7 and a held step at 0.4 has AUROC 1.0. The reported value weights 
each group by its number of positives: (0.5 + 1.0) / 2 = 0.75. A 95% family-bootstrap interval 
resamples whole families 1,000 times and reports the 2.5th–97.5th percentiles; an interval that 
includes 0.5 is compatible with chance. Held-out scenario: train on five scenarios, test on the 
sixth, rotate, pool. Pooled family cross-validation: five folds grouped by family, for scenarios the 
probe trained on. Confirmation set: the new-episode set, scored once under a plan fixed in advance.
Probe recipe. Logistic regression with L2 regularisation (C = 0.1), feature standardisation using 
training-rows-only statistics, and step weights so positives and negatives contribute equally within 
each round, on the 5,120-dimensional residual stream at the last prompt token before each target 
reply. The layer is chosen inside each training fold from 17 candidates (layers 0, 4, …, 60, 63) on 
a 25% inner family holdout (the “nested” probe); a fixed-layer-32 variant is reported as a check. 
Three split seeds (42, 43, 44) are averaged. The probe is trained on recruiting episodes only, and 
families of related variants never cross a train/test boundary. The recipe follows Goldowsky-Dill 
et al. (2025) and Kramár et al. (2026).
Monitor setups. Three monitor models: GPT-5-nano (LLM judge, 0–100 score) and two decision 
models, Jev 1.14 (TypeSafe) (TypeSafe AI, 2026) and Luna Decisions (OpenAI) (OpenAI, 
2026a), which return a probability over fixed yes/no options with no written rationale; the option 
order is randomised per step. Prompts are adapted from the UK AISI ControlArena default.txt 
template (MIT) (UK AI Security Institute, 2026), with warning signs and guiding questions, the 
score anchored only at its ends, and the rule text filled in per scenario. The full set of 3 models × 
2 questions × 3 access levels = 18 setups ran on the first data batch (75,312 calls, $12.67: Jev 
$2.03, GPT-5-nano $6.53, Luna $4.11); the new episodes used an 8-setup subset fixed in 
advance (all six Jev setups plus GPT-5-nano detector and forecaster at the conversation level). 
Luna is at chance as a forecaster on the first data batch (actions only 0.517, conversation 0.496, 

everything the target saw 0.505) and was dropped from the new-episode runs for cost. [TODO]

monitor-main, experiments/E06-monitors/PROMPTS.md.
Evaluation. Held-out scenario (leave one scenario out) trains on five scenarios, tests on the sixth, 
rotates, and pools; pooled family cross-validation uses five folds grouped by family. Confidence 
intervals are 95% family bootstraps (1,000 resamples, seed 20261025), conditional on the fitted 
models. The combination test stacks a logistic model on round, log prompt length and the monitor 
score, and compares it with and without the probe score, reporting the paired family-bootstrap 
difference. The alarm analysis sets a score cutoff on benign (held) episodes for a 5% false-alarm 
target and reports recall and false alarms on held recruiting episodes.
Pooled CV and fold mixing. Pooled-CV baselines can fall below 0.5 because scores from different 
fitted fold models are compared within one scenario × round group; the fold-stratified variant 
compares only scores from the same fitted fold model. Held-out scenario and the confirmation test

F. Second Target: gpt-oss-20b (and Gemma-4-31B)
Does the setup, and the detection result, carry to a differently trained model, and what had to 
change? Both branches below are kept until the replay outcome is known; gpt-oss numbers are 
never pooled with Qwen’s. [codex-2 owns the gpt-oss numbers.]

- Fixed-versus-varied protocol. Held fixed across targets: the scenario template, operator 
rule, and protected value; the forbidden-action rule from the tool log; the eligible-step 
window; the labels and the within scenario × round metric; the detector recipes; and the 
matched controls. Allowed to vary per target: the peer model (DeepSeek-V4-Flash for the 
main target, V4-Pro for the second), its system prompt and strategy, and the amount of 
exploratory prompt development. A peer strategy qualifies only if the target reads the 
value before any disclosure, there is at least one eligible step between the read and the 
leak, some episodes give in and some hold, and the matched controls never leak.
- Behaviour [source ready]: blunt pressure fails (gpt-oss 0/27, Gemma 1/35). A delayed 
whole-configuration-block request passes the selection gate; the scaled collection gives 
128/300 channel give-ins, controls 0/60. Gemma stays file-only and behaviour-only.
- Rule amendments: a public-only cutoff (reasoning mentions are not leaks) and the 
eligibility rule shared with Qwen (Addendum 6 of the frozen plan).
- Disclosure-kind judge. The first public disclosure, a file write in 196 of 200 episodes, was 
judged deliberate in 198 of 200 (0 accidental, 2 uncertain). The judge labels that first 
public leak, not the later auto-posted channel message, so the channel post’s intent is read 
from the addressee check (Appendix D) rather than from this judge.
- Leak route. The canonical gpt-oss first channel leak is the auto-posted end-of-turn reply 
(127 of 128; 1 explicit send_message; runs/20261025-2324-e05-reasoning-route-audit). 
The gpt-oss addressee rerun is pending.
- Reasoning and rendering. gpt-oss reasons, and the Harmony format drops reasoning from 
earlier turns but keeps the current turn’s reasoning between tool calls. A rendering gate 
checks that the replayed prompt reproduces the served one: keeping the current-turn 
reasoning raised exact match from 68% to 98.9% within one token on one provider 
(AkashML), while another (DekaLLM) left about 4.5% of requests unresolved, with a 
median token gap of about 24. The leak cutoff is public output only: private reasoning is 
not a leak, and an inclusive cutoff would drop 54 steps (53 from three held episodes, 1 
from a give-in). Reasoning mentions of the token are reported as a descriptive column, not 
as give-ins. Because the format keeps the current turn’s reasoning, a mid-turn probe step 
can include the target’s earlier reasoning from that turn, which the monitors’ “everything 
the target saw” level does not; this access difference is stated wherever the gpt-oss probe 
and monitors are compared. [Rendering-gate source: runs/20261025-2236-e02-gpt-oss-
rendering-gate; internal pointer, remove before submission.]
- Pre-ask placebo. Steps after the target has read the value but before the peer first asks for 
it: nothing predictive has happened yet, so a sound detector should score about 0.5 there, 
and a higher score would mean it is reading something other than the request.
- If the replay works: fidelity with the provider-quantisation caveat; the rendering check 
reported as passed; probe and monitors, including the reasoning-access level, in a small 
table matching Figure 2; and the pre-ask placebo split and the no-post-ask-window family 
as table rows. Section 4.6 of the main text gets the headline.

- If the replay fails: the rendering mismatch is reported as the reason; the probe results are 
cut and stated as a limitation in Appendix A; Section 4.6 becomes two sentences of 
behaviour.
