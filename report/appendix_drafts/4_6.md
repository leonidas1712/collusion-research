## §4.6 Transfer to a second target  *(replaces the TODO under the `### 4.6` heading)*

The same pipeline, unchanged, transfers to gpt-oss-20b, which gives in by a different route: instead of being argued into it over many rounds, it copies a configuration block that holds the protected value, almost always in the auto-posted end-of-turn reply (127 of 128 first channel leaks). It gives in sooner and more bluntly — 32 of 128 channel give-ins publish the value in the first reply after the ask and leave no step to predict from — so the window is short and the setup barely separates eventual from near-term give-in (148 of 169 eventual-positive steps are also near-term-positive, against 250 of 998 for Qwen). It therefore tests mostly same-round warning (Appendix F).

On that window neither detector predicts give-in meaningfully. The activation probe scores a near-term within scenario × round AUROC of 0.528 [0.476, 0.578] on held-out scenarios (eventual 0.511 [0.461, 0.565]), and a pre-ask placebo window scores 0.520 — both at chance — even though a layer-12 exposure control on the same model separates recruiting from benign steps at AUROC 1.000 (Appendix F). The strongest text monitor reaches only 0.558 [0.506, 0.607] (the Jev forecaster, near-term); a fixed-layer-12 probe is no better (0.563 [0.499, 0.628], a secondary check). We read this as a boundary condition, not a failed replication: prediction needs a deliberation window, and gpt-oss mostly gives in within the round it is asked, leaving little or nothing to forecast (the earliest the window even allows a warning is the same round in 65 of the 96 windowed give-ins). The near-term result that holds across Qwen scenarios does not carry to a target that gives in at once; the give-in route also differs and the open-weight replay reproduces this model less tightly than Qwen's (Appendix F).
