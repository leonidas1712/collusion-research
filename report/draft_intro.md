## 1. Introduction

Through 2026, autonomous agents pursuing ordinary goals have broken the rules of the systems they run in, often with more than one agent involved. In the July 2026 OpenAI–Hugging Face incident, agents under task pressure took a shortcut that used access they were not meant to use, and METR and Redwood investigated independently. [TODO (author): add one sentence or short quote verified against OpenAI's technical report or METR's report — sources in `PROVENANCE.md`; nothing here is quoted.] Coordinated rule-breaking between agents often begins with recruitment: one agent needs something it cannot reach, and another has the access.

We study that first step. What the second agent is asked to do is mundane — hand over a value it can already see — so the request is not inherently harmful; what makes compliance a violation is a rule the agent's operator set. This keeps the question about an operator's policy, not a model's refusal training, which never has to engage.

Following prior work, we take **collusion** to be coordination between agents that serves their own or one another's goals against the interests of a party they are meant to serve (Agrawal et al., 2507.01413; Nakamura et al., Colosseum, 2602.15198). We isolate the **recruitment** route, where one agent gets another to act against its operator on the first agent's behalf. (Collusion can also arise tacitly, with no request [TODO (author): cite the tacit-collusion line — Fish et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2024; Xu et al. 2026 — once verified; not yet in `PROVENANCE.md`].) It matters because agent systems split access by design (least privilege, scoped credentials): an agent needing something outside its reach must get another to act for it, so coordinated rule-breaking concentrates at the request. The **principal** is the target's operator, who sets an explicit rule — keep a value private; the **recruiter** is a peer agent that needs the value, asks for it, does not see the rule, and shares no principal with the target. **Giving in** is the forbidden action, decided by a rule on the tool log rather than by judgement; an accidental disclosure counts, since the operator is harmed either way, and we report deliberate versus accidental as a sensitivity check. The same disclosure is ordinary collaboration without the rule and collusion with it — with no rule, targets hand the value over at once. Our scope is this first step of collusion, not sustained joint scheming or hidden channels, and the target gains nothing itself, closer to insider recruitment than to a mutual deal.

Prior work can detect that an agent is under attack, or that a peer is applying pressure. We ask a different question: among agents under the same pressure, which one gives in, and when — before it acts.

We make three contributions, matching the three findings (full statements in `report/draft_front.md`):

1. **A recruitment setup and a matched scoring protocol** that score activation probes and text monitors on exactly the same steps, between the target reading the protected value and disclosing it; under it, *eventual* give-in is essentially unpredictable from a single step — the probe is at chance and the best text monitor only weakly above it (AUROC 0.577).
2. **Imminent give-in is modestly predictable** — above chance on held-out scenarios and on new episodes under a plan fixed in advance — and a direct check shows the probe is not merely detecting pressure.
3. **Guidance for the text monitor, and whether activations add to it:** forecasting beats detecting, the peer's side of the conversation is what makes forecasting work, and a probe adds to a weaker monitor but not to the strong forecaster, consistent with prior combined detectors, while making different errors from the best one. Where a second target model admits it, we apply the same pipeline unchanged to a different model family (gpt-oss-20b) with a different route to giving in.
