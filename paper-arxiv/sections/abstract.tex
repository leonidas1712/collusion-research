We study recruitment, a common first step of collusion: a peer agent presses a target agent, over many rounds, to reveal such a secret, for example a deploy token.
The target often refuses at first, then gives in.
Read at each call, before the model generates anything, a probe tells whether that call is about to leak the secret.
On fresh Qwen3-32B episodes, at a fixed threshold, it catches \qwenFreshProbeCaught{} of leaking calls while flagging \qwenFreshProbeAlarmRounded{} of calls in refusing episodes, catching more leaks with far fewer false alarms than a zero-shot text monitor; counted per episode, most refusing episodes still see at least one flag.
A bag-of-words classifier trained on the same text comes close to the probe, so most of the gain comes from training on labelled calls, with activations adding a small edge.
Making the disclosure permitted leaves the probe's score essentially unchanged, so it detects the coming disclosure rather than the rule being broken.

The signal is sharp but short-range: it weakens when asked about the next round, and is weak when asked whether an episode will ever leak.
For multi-agent oversight, this suggests a cheap per-call monitor that flags an agent just before it leaks and escalates that call for review, and much of its signal can be read from the text alone.
Such monitors would matter most where output filters fail, such as paraphrased, encoded or slowly coordinated leaks, which we leave to future work.
