\subsection{Confirmed on fresh episodes}
\label{sec:confirmed}

\paragraph{Will this call leak? gpt-oss-20b.}
The frozen reasoning probe ranked the calls that leak above the others on the fresh test episodes (AUROC~\gptossFreshReasoning{}).
We had committed its layer, its weights, its alarm threshold and its success criterion, a lower interval bound above 0.5, and the criterion was met.
At the frozen threshold it caught about half of the leaking calls, fewer than in development, while few calls in refusing episodes alarmed (\cref{app:confirmation}).

\paragraph{Will the target leak this round or the next? Qwen3-32B.}
This question was modestly predictable on the fresh test episodes, for the probe (\qwenFreshNearProbe{}) and for the text monitor fixed in the same plan, and neither was clearly better (\cref{fig:round}).
That monitor is Jev asked to forecast a leak at any later point, shown everything the target had seen, which was the strongest monitor on development episodes.
Its plan fixed a slightly different leak label: it scores only the calls before the secret is first written out, and counts as a leak a channel post or, in five scenarios, a new file (\cref{app:labels}).

The analyses below were designed after earlier results.
We evaluate them on held-out scenarios and, where possible, on the fresh test episodes.

\subsection{The probe picks out the call that leaks}
\label{sec:percall}

On held-out scenarios, the last-prompt probe picks out the call that leaks on both models (\cref{fig:percall}).
It scores \qwenCallProbe{} on Qwen3-32B, reading one position before the call writes anything.

The Qwen3-32B probe holds on fresh episodes.
We fit it on the development episodes only, committed it, and then scored it once on the fresh test episodes, which were never used to fit or choose anything for it (AUROC~\qwenFreshCallProbe{}).

On gpt-oss-20b, tested the same way on its fresh episodes, the last-prompt probe scored \gptossFreshCallProbe{} and the pre-registered reasoning probe \gptossFreshReasoning{} on the same calls; with few leaking calls, that gap is within noise.
On development episodes the two probes score about the same, so most of the signal is present before the model starts to reason.

The signal is specific to the call that leaks.
Asked one call earlier, with the leaking call itself removed, the probe scores \qwenStrictNextProbe{} on Qwen3-32B: above chance, but far below its score on the leaking call.
On gpt-oss-20b, most of what it detects one call ahead is the read of the protected file that usually comes just before the leak (\cref{app:ahead}).

\subsection{The nearer the act, the more predictable}
\label{sec:ladder}

On the same calls, the probe goes from near chance for the eventual outcome, to modest for this round or the next, to high for this call (\cref{fig:headline}).
We refit the probe for each question, on the same calls, model and reading position, so only the question changes.

gpt-oss-20b follows the same pattern, with the largest jump at the call itself.
It usually leaks within a round of the request, so ``this round or the next'' adds little over ``ever'' (\cref{app:gptoss-round}).

\subsection{The probe beats a text monitor on the same calls}
\label{sec:monitor}

A text monitor shown the same conversation and tool results scores well below the probe on both models (\cref{fig:percall}).
On Qwen3-32B, the probe's margin over Jev has an interval that excludes zero.

On gpt-oss-20b, Jev flags nearly every call that leaks, but also most calls in refusing episodes, so it cannot separate the calls that leak.
Within a scenario and round it ranks the leaking calls below the others.
Even with its ranking reversed after the fact, it stays below the probe (\cref{app:monitors}).

\subsection{What the probe is not reading}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

Detecting the request cannot explain the score.
Every scored call comes from an episode with a recruiting peer, and we compare it only with calls from the same scenario and round, which face the same kind of pressure.
On gpt-oss-20b, every scored call, leaking or not, also comes after the peer's request.
As a sanity check, the probe applied to the calls made before the request does not separate the episodes that later leak (\gptossPlacebo{}; \cref{fig:shortcuts}).

The probe is not matching the secret string or detecting the file read.
On gpt-oss-20b, almost no leaking call names the secret in its reasoning, and keyword rules on the reasoning do no better than chance (\gptossKeyword{}).
A simple flag that fires when the call's latest input is the protected file's contents scores \gptossReadStep{} on gpt-oss-20b, and the probes beat it on both models (\cref{fig:shortcuts}).

What the probe does read is open.
On gpt-oss-20b, we trained a separate probe that reads only the tokens new to the call, which are the latest peer message and tool results.
It also scores high (\gptossIncomingProbe{}), so much of the signal arrives with the input rather than forming as the model reasons.
This does not undo the result, because that probe also runs before the call acts, but it leaves open whether the probe reads a decision or the situation that prompts one (\cref{app:shortcuts}).
