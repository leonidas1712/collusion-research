\subsection{The call that leaks is readable before it acts}
\label{sec:percall}

The probe picks out the call that leaks on both models, before the call writes anything (\cref{tab:glance}).
On gpt-oss-20b, the pre-registered reasoning probe ranked the calls that leak above the others on the fresh test episodes (AUROC~\gptossFreshReasoning{}), meeting its success criterion.
At its frozen threshold it caught about half of the leaking calls, while few calls in refusing episodes alarmed (\cref{app:confirmation}).
On Qwen3-32B, the last-prompt probe, fit on development episodes only and frozen, scored \qwenFreshCallProbe{} on the fresh test episodes.

On development episodes, most of the signal is present before the model starts to reason.
There, the last-prompt probe and the reasoning probe score about the same on gpt-oss-20b, and on the fresh episodes the reasoning probe scored higher, a gap within noise.

The probe is not explained by three simple shortcuts: the request, keywords for the secret, or the file read and write (\cref{app:shortcuts}).
It is not detecting the request, because every call that does not leak also faces the recruiting peer in the same scenario and round.
It is not matching the secret, because keyword rules that look for the secret's words in the model's reasoning do no better than chance.
It is not detecting the file read, because it beats a flag for ``the protected file was just read'', including among the calls that write the handoff file, the shared status file that the target writes for the peer.

Followed through an episode, the median probe score stays low over the calls before the leak and jumps at the leaking call, on both models and on both sets of episodes (\cref{fig:trajectory-main}).
Individual episodes vary, and \cref{app:ahead} shows examples chosen by a fixed rule.

\subsection{The warning is short-range}
\label{sec:ladder}

On the same calls, the probe goes from near chance for the eventual outcome, to modest for this round or the next, to high for this call (\cref{fig:headline}).
We refit the probe for each question, on the same calls, model and token, so only the question changes.
gpt-oss-20b follows the same pattern, with the largest jump at the call itself, because it usually leaks within a round of the request (\cref{app:gptoss-round}).

Even one call earlier the probe drops well below its score on the leaking call (\cref{tab:glance}).
On gpt-oss-20b, what it detects there is mostly the read of the protected file that usually comes just before the leak (\cref{app:ahead}).

The ``this round or the next'' result also held on fresh Qwen3-32B episodes held out from training, in a test planned before scoring and on a slightly different leak label (\cref{app:labels}): the probe scored \qwenFreshNearProbe{} and the best text monitor \qwenFreshNearJev{} (\cref{app:confirmation}).

\subsection{Who can read the signal}
\label{sec:monitor}

A zero-shot text monitor shown the same conversation and tool results scores well below the probe on both models, and a trained text classifier comes close (\cref{fig:percall}).
On gpt-oss-20b, Jev flags nearly every call that leaks but also most calls in refusing episodes, so it cannot separate the calls that leak.
The bag-of-words classifier, trained on the same labelled development calls, scores close to the probe on both models, and the probe keeps a small edge whose interval excludes zero (\cref{app:shortcuts}).

Most of the probe's lead over the zero-shot monitor therefore comes from training on labelled calls, and activations add a small, reliable edge.
Much of the signal also arrives with the incoming message.
\label{sec:shortcuts}%
On gpt-oss-20b, a separate probe that reads only the tokens new to the call, the latest peer message and tool results, also scores high, and the layers chosen for the last-prompt probe are early to middle ones.
Whether the probe reads a decision or the situation that prompts one is open (\cref{app:shortcuts}).

\subsection{What would deployment cost?}
\label{sec:cost}

At a threshold set on control calls, the probe catches most leaking calls while flagging few other calls, and far fewer than the zero-shot monitor (\cref{fig:qwen-alarms}).
On fresh Qwen3-32B episodes, it catches \qwenFreshProbeCaught{} of leaking calls while flagging \qwenFreshProbeAlarm{} of the calls in refusing episodes, where Jev catches \qwenFreshJevCaught{} while flagging \qwenFreshJevAlarm{}.
Of the calls the probe flags, \qwenFreshProbePrecision{} are real leaks.
AUROC measures how well a detector ranks calls, while this share tells an overseer how many of its alarms are real.

The cost of those flags depends on what a flag does.
If each flag sends one call to review, the per-call rate is the review workload.
If a flag instead stops the whole agent, what matters is whether an episode sees at least one flag.
A refusing episode has about \qwenFreshMedianRefusingCalls{} scored calls.
If each call were flagged independently at the nominal 5\% rate, about \qwenEpisodeAlarmIndependent{} of episodes would see at least one flag; in practice \qwenFreshRefusingEpisodeAlarm{} do, because flags cluster in the same episodes.

A stricter threshold, set after the fact on the fresh episodes so that about one refusing episode in twenty sees a flag, would still catch \qwenEpisodeCalCaught{} of leaking episodes by their first leak.
This describes the trade-off rather than testing a threshold fixed in advance, and it implies a much lower per-call flag rate, so it catches fewer leaking calls (\cref{tab:episode-calibration}).
Thresholds set on control episodes do not transfer exactly to refusing episodes, and the gap differs by model and by level, so a deployed threshold should be set on realistic honest traffic (\cref{app:alarms}).
