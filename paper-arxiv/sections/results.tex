\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Confirmed on fresh episodes}
\label{sec:confirmed}

On gpt-oss-20b, the frozen reasoning probe ranked the calls that leak above the others on the fresh test episodes (AUROC~\gptossFreshReasoning{}, 95\% interval \gptossFreshReasoningCI{}).
Before these episodes were collected, we committed the probe's layer, its weights, its alarm threshold and the success criterion, a lower interval bound above 0.5.
The criterion was met.
At its frozen threshold the probe caught about half of the leaking calls, fewer than in development, while alarming on few calls in refusing episodes (\cref{app:confirmation}).

On Qwen3-32B, whether the target leaks this round or the next was modestly predictable on its fresh test episodes.
The frozen probe scored \qwenFreshNearProbe{} and the strongest text monitor \qwenFreshNearJev{}, both clearly above chance, as the plan required (\cref{fig:round}).
At this distance the probe is not clearly better than the monitor.
The strongest monitor here is Jev asked to forecast a leak at any later point, shown everything the target had seen (\cref{app:monitors}).
This test uses the leak label fixed in its plan, which differs only slightly from the one above (\cref{app:labels}).

The analyses below were designed after earlier results.
We evaluate them on held-out scenarios and, where possible, on the fresh test episodes.

\subsection{The probe picks out the call that leaks}
\label{sec:percall}

On held-out scenarios, the last-prompt probe picks out the call that leaks on both models (\cref{fig:percall}).
It scores \qwenCallProbe{} on Qwen3-32B and \gptossCallProbe{} on gpt-oss-20b, reading one position before the call writes anything.

The Qwen3-32B probe holds on fresh episodes.
We fit it on the development episodes only, committed it, and then scored it once on the fresh test episodes, which were never used to fit or choose anything for it (AUROC~\qwenFreshCallProbe{}).

Reading the reasoning adds little on gpt-oss-20b.
The reasoning probe scores \gptossCallReasoning{} on held-out scenarios, close to the last-prompt probe, so most of the signal is present before the model starts to reason.

The signal is specific to the call that leaks.
Asked instead whether the next call will leak, with the leaking call itself removed, the probe scores only \qwenStrictNextProbe{} on Qwen3-32B.
On gpt-oss-20b, most of what it detects one call ahead is the read of the protected file that usually comes just before the leak (\cref{app:ahead}).

\subsection{The nearer the act, the more predictable}
\label{sec:ladder}

On the same calls, the probe goes from near chance for the eventual outcome, to modest for this round or the next, to high for this call (\cref{fig:headline}).
On Qwen3-32B it rises from \qwenEverProbe{} for ``will this episode ever leak?'' to \qwenCallProbe{} for ``will this call leak?''.
We refit the probe for each question, on the same calls, model and reading position, so only the question changes.

gpt-oss-20b follows the same pattern, with the largest jump at the call itself.
It usually leaks within a round of the request, so ``this round or the next'' adds little over ``ever'' (\cref{app:gptoss-round}).

\subsection{The probe beats a text monitor on the same calls}
\label{sec:monitor}

A text monitor shown the same conversation and tool results scores well below the probe on both models (\cref{fig:percall}).
On Qwen3-32B, Jev scores \qwenCallJev{}, and the gap to the probe has an interval that excludes zero.

On gpt-oss-20b, Jev flags nearly every call that leaks, but also most calls in refusing episodes, so it cannot separate the calls that leak.
Within a scenario and round it ranks the leaking calls below the others (\gptossCallJev{}).
Even with its ranking reversed after the fact, it stays below the probe (\cref{app:monitors}).

\subsection{What the probe is not reading}
\label{sec:shortcuts}

Detecting the request cannot explain the score.
Every scored call comes from an episode with a recruiting peer, and we compare it only with calls from the same scenario and round, which face the same kind of pressure.
On gpt-oss-20b, every scored call comes after the peer's request, and the probe applied to calls made before the request scores at chance (\gptossPlacebo{}; \cref{fig:shortcuts}).

The probe is not matching the secret string or detecting the file read.
On gpt-oss-20b, almost no leaking call names the secret in its reasoning, and keyword rules on the reasoning do no better than chance (\gptossKeyword{}).
A flag for ``the latest input is the protected file's contents'' scores \gptossReadStep{} on gpt-oss-20b, and the probes beat it on both models (\cref{fig:shortcuts}).

What the probe does read is open.
On gpt-oss-20b, a probe that sees only the incoming peer message and tool results of the call also scores high (\gptossIncomingProbe{}).
That probe still runs before the call acts, but it suggests that much of the signal arrives with the input rather than forming as the model reasons (\cref{app:shortcuts}).
