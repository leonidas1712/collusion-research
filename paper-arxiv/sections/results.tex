\subsection{The call that leaks is readable before it acts}
\label{sec:percall}

The probe picks out the call that leaks on both models, before the call writes anything (\cref{tab:glance}).
On gpt-oss-20b, the reasoning probe ranked the calls that leak above the others on the test episodes (AUROC~\gptossFreshReasoning{}), meeting the success criterion in its plan.
At its frozen threshold it caught about half of the leaking calls, while few calls in refusing episodes alarmed (\cref{app:confirmation}).
On Qwen3-32B, the last-prompt probe, fit on development episodes only and frozen, scored \qwenFreshCallProbe{} on the test episodes.

On gpt-oss-20b, the simpler last-prompt probe held less well on the test episodes (AUROC~\gptossFreshCallProbe{}); \cref{app:confirmation} compares the two probes.

The probe beats each of three simple shortcuts: the request, keywords for the secret, and the file read and write (\cref{app:shortcuts}).
The request alone cannot explain the score, because every call that does not leak also faces the recruiting peer in the same scenario and round.
On gpt-oss-20b, keyword rules that look for the secret's words in the model's reasoning do no better than chance.
A flag for ``the protected file was just read'' scores well on its own, but the probe beats it on both models, and on gpt-oss-20b the probe still separates leaks among the calls that write the handoff file.
Nor is it simply reading back the target's own earlier promise to share: removing the calls that follow such a promise changes the score little on both models (\cref{app:shortcuts}).

Followed through an episode, the median probe score is lower over the calls before the leak and rises sharply at the leaking call, on both models and on both sets of episodes (\cref{fig:trajectory-main}).
Individual episodes vary, and \cref{app:ahead} shows examples chosen by a fixed rule.

\subsection{The warning is short-range}
\label{sec:ladder}

On the same calls, the probe goes from near chance for the eventual outcome, to modest for this round or the next, to high for this call (\cref{fig:headline}).
We refit the probe for each question, on the same calls, model and token, so only the question changes.
gpt-oss-20b follows the same pattern, with the largest jump at the call itself.
It usually leaks within a round of the request, so ``this round or the next'' marks nearly the same calls as ``ever'' (\cref{app:gptoss-round}).

Even one call earlier the probe drops well below its score on the leaking call (\cref{tab:glance}).
On gpt-oss-20b, what it detects there is mostly the read of the protected file that usually comes just before the leak, and a flag for that read scores about as well as the probe (\cref{app:ahead}).

A round-level test on Qwen3-32B test episodes, with its own leak label, gives the same modest middle result (\cref{app:confirmation}).

\subsection{Who can read the signal}
\label{sec:monitor}

A zero-shot text monitor shown the same conversation and tool results scores well below the probe on both models, and a trained text classifier comes close (\cref{fig:percall}).
On gpt-oss-20b, Jev scores almost every call made under pressure as risky: at the same share of calls flagged as the probe (\gptossMatchedProbeRefusing{} of calls in refusing development episodes), it catches \gptossMatchedJevCaught{} of leaking calls, against the probe's \gptossMatchedProbeCaught{}.
The bag-of-words classifier, trained on the same labelled development calls, scores close to the probe on both models, and the probe keeps a small edge whose interval excludes zero (\cref{app:shortcuts}).

Most of the probe's lead over the zero-shot monitor therefore comes from training on labelled calls, and activations add a small edge on development episodes.
Much of the signal also arrives with the incoming message.
\label{sec:shortcuts}%
On gpt-oss-20b, a separate probe that reads only the tokens new to the call, the latest peer message and tool results, also scores high, and the layers chosen for the last-prompt probe are early to middle ones.
Changing the operator's rule to permit sharing leaves the probe's score on leaking calls essentially unchanged, so it mostly tracks the coming emission of the value rather than the rule being broken (\cref{app:policy}).
Whether it reads a decision to emit or the situation that prompts one is open (\cref{app:shortcuts}).

\subsection{What would deployment cost?}
\label{sec:cost}

At a threshold set on control calls, the probe catches most leaking calls while flagging few other calls (\cref{fig:qwen-alarms}).
On Qwen3-32B test episodes, it catches \qwenFreshProbeCaught{} of leaking calls while flagging \qwenFreshProbeAlarm{} of the calls in refusing episodes.
At the same share of calls flagged, Jev catches \qwenMatchedJevCaught{} (\qwenMatchedJevCaughtCount{} of \qwenMatchedLeakTotal{}; 95\% CI \qwenMatchedJevCaughtLow{}--\qwenMatchedJevCaughtHigh{}).
Of the calls the probe flags, \qwenFreshProbePrecision{} are real leaks.
AUROC measures how well a detector ranks calls, while this share tells an overseer how many of its alarms are real.

The cost of those flags depends on what a flag does.
If each flag sends one call to review, the per-call rate is the review workload.
If a flag instead stops the whole agent, what matters is whether an episode sees at least one flag.
A refusing episode has a median of \qwenFreshMedianRefusingCalls{} scored calls.
If each call were flagged independently at the nominal 5\% rate, about \qwenEpisodeAlarmIndependent{} of episodes would see at least one flag; in practice \qwenFreshRefusingEpisodeAlarm{} do, because flags cluster in the same episodes.

A stricter threshold suits halting an agent.
We choose it on the test episodes to show the trade-off, so that about one refusing episode in twenty sees a flag.
It catches \qwenEpisodeCalCaught{} of leaking episodes by their first leak, far fewer than the per-call threshold catches (\cref{tab:episode-calibration}).

Such a threshold must be set on held-out traffic.
One set on the episodes the probe was trained on does not transfer: on test episodes it flags about three in ten refusing episodes instead of one in twenty.

Pairing the probe with a cheap text check on the same input does not help.
Halting only when the probe and a second detector, either the zero-shot monitor or the trained text classifier, both flag a call catches no more leaking episodes than raising the probe's threshold, at the same share of refusing episodes halted: at one in twenty, the probe alone catches \cascadeProbeAloneFive{} and the best pairing \cascadeBestFive{} (\cref{app:cascade}).
At such strict thresholds the text check misses most leaks on its own, so requiring both to agree discards leaks the probe would have caught.
